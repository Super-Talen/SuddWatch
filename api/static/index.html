    Why early warning matters

    Since 2019, South Sudan has had four years of severe flooding along the Nile and in the Sudd wetland; in 2021 Unity State saw its worst floods in 60 years. In our own satellite record, the share of county-periods above their normal high-water level rose from 19% in 2017–2023 to 37% from mid-2025. Floods cut off roads, clinics and farms for months. A warning two to four weeks ahead gives time to move supplies and people.

    What the satellite sees

    Satellite images of Bentiu, the capital of Unity State, where the floods of 2021 left the town surrounded by water for years. SuddWatch watches the same landscape with Sentinel-1 radar, which sees through clouds, so it can measure water in every county during the rainy season too.

    How SuddWatch works

    1. 1

      Observe

      Every 12 days, Sentinel-1 radar measures how much of each county is covered by water. Rainfall (CHIRPS) and terrain (elevation, slope, height above the nearest river) add context.

    2. 2

      Learn

      A gradient-boosted tree model learned from 2017–2023 which conditions come before a county's water rises above its own normal level. It only ever sees information available on the forecast date.

    3. 3

      Warn

      The model gives each county a calibrated probability. Counties above the alert threshold are flagged. The threshold was set so that no more than 15% of non-flood cases raise a false alarm.

    Honest comparison: simply assuming "flooded now means flooded in two weeks" already scores 0.919 on the same test period. Floods in the Sudd last for months, so the model's main gain is catching counties that are about to change, and doing so with reliable probabilities.
